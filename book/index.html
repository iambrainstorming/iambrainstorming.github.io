Most Web3 projects begin with a token.

We are beginning with a question:

What would actual decentralization look like if we built it around something people use every day—food, health, knowledge, and community?

That is why we are building SmartPushti.

SmartPushti is not designed to be another speculative token or meme coin. The token is not the product. The community is the product. The protocol is the experiment. And governance is at the center of it.

Our goal is to explore whether decentralized technology can help communities collectively create knowledge, establish reputation, fund useful work, and make decisions without depending entirely on a centralized company or platform.

Building an Open Health Care Ecosystem 🌿

SmartPushti is building an open, community-driven ecosystem for health and wellness.

Our vision is to create a platform where people can contribute knowledge, earn reputation, support useful ideas, and collectively decide what deserves attention and funding through transparent community governance.

Rather than keeping health knowledge isolated within institutions, we want to connect researchers, clinicians, educators, patients, and local communities on open, decentralized infrastructure. Health research, evidence-based discussions, and community knowledge should be accessible to everyone, not confined to a few organizations.

The ecosystem will grow across many areas of health and wellness, including:

  • Nutrition
  • Fitness
  • Mental wellbeing
  • Preventive care
  • Healthy cooking
  • Health research
  • Evidence-based discussions
  • Community knowledge

These conversations and publications will take place through open, decentralized infrastructure such as Nostr, using a Nostr application we are building.

Bridging the Gap Between Health Researchers and the Public

Health care research should not be isolated from the public. Many important discoveries remain locked behind academic journals, institutional silos, or technical communities, making it difficult for ordinary people to understand, contribute to, or benefit from ongoing research. Yet patients, caregivers, citizen scientists, and local communities often possess valuable knowledge, experiences, and observations that can accelerate scientific progress.

SmartPushti, built on the Nostr protocol, aims to bridge the gap between researchers and the public by making health care publishing more open, collaborative, and community-driven. Researchers can share findings, discuss hypotheses, publish datasets, and receive feedback in an open environment, while the public can ask questions, share lived experiences, contribute local knowledge, and participate in meaningful discussions. This creates a continuous exchange of ideas rather than a one-way flow of information.

The platform is designed with modular communication in mind. Different applications can provide different views, filters, moderation policies, and curated lists tailored to their users. A scientist may prefer highly technical discussions with peer researchers, while a patient community may focus on practical health experiences and accessible explanations. Local communities can build their own feeds, lists, and moderation systems around specific diseases, regions, languages, or research topics. Every community can tailor its own experience without fragmenting the underlying network.

This flexibility allows the same open protocol to support many different forms of collaboration. Whether you are a researcher, clinician, educator, patient, caregiver, or citizen scientist, you can choose or create the interface that best fits your needs. Instead of forcing everyone into a single social feed, SmartPushti enables many local views over the same shared knowledge graph.

Why Conviction Voting?

One of the most important experiments inside SmartPushti is conviction voting.

Traditional voting asks:

“What do you support right now?”

Conviction voting asks something deeper:

“How strongly do you support this, and how long are you willing to commit to it?”

That difference is extremely important for decentralized governance.

In a conventional one-person-one-vote system, a vote is usually a single moment. You can vote for a proposal and walk away.

SmartPushti experiments with a model where participants commit tokens to their convictions. The longer-term and stronger the commitment, the greater the potential influence.

In our current design, participants create a conviction by choosing a conviction level and staking tokens. Voting weight is determined by the relationship between the stake and conviction level, while higher conviction also requires a longer lock period.

For example, someone could stake 100 PUSHTI with conviction level 5, creating 500 units of voting weight. Another participant could stake 50 PUSHTI with conviction level 10, also creating 500 units of voting weight—but with a longer commitment.

The point isn’t simply to give people more voting power.

The point is to make influence costly to fake.

Commitment Over Speculation

Imagine two people.

One person buys tokens because they think the price might increase tomorrow.

Another person uses those tokens to support a community proposal they genuinely believe will improve the ecosystem—and is willing to lock those tokens for a longer period.

Should those two people have exactly the same governance influence?

We don’t think that is necessarily the best model.

Conviction voting introduces time and commitment into governance.

A participant who wants significant influence has to demonstrate more than ownership. They have to demonstrate commitment.

That creates an interesting incentive:

Short-term speculation becomes less useful for governance, while long-term participation becomes more valuable.

The SmartPushti design specifically uses lock durations, conviction resets, vote limits, and activity-based mechanisms to make short-term manipulation more difficult.

Of course, conviction voting is not a magical solution to governance.

It doesn’t eliminate whales.

It doesn’t guarantee good decisions.

It doesn’t make every voter rational.

It is an experiment in finding a better relationship between ownership, participation, time, and influence.

Why Reputation Matters

Decentralization isn’t only about who owns tokens.

It is also about who the community trusts.

The internet has created enormous amounts of content, but follower counts, likes, and popularity aren’t necessarily good measures of quality.

A viral recipe isn’t necessarily nutritious.

A popular health claim isn’t necessarily scientifically accurate.

A large account isn’t necessarily a trustworthy contributor.

SmartPushti therefore experiments with decentralized reputation.

People can contribute recipes, food knowledge, health information, research, and other useful work. The community can evaluate those contributions, and consistent participation can help build reputation.

Conviction voting adds another dimension: people can demonstrate that they are willing to commit to their evaluations rather than simply clicking a button.

Over time, the combination of contribution, reputation, and conviction can create a richer picture of who is consistently contributing value to the ecosystem.

Conviction as Community Funding

This becomes especially interesting when we connect conviction voting to funding.

Imagine someone proposes:

  • A community nutrition project
  • A new healthy recipe initiative
  • Local food-sharing infrastructure
  • Nutrition research
  • An open-source health tool
  • A fitness education project
  • A mental-wellness resource
  • A local community kitchen

Instead of asking a centralized company whether the idea deserves funding, the community can evaluate it.

Participants can assign scores and commit their conviction.

In the current SmartPushti design, proposals are evaluated on a 1–10 scale, with scores weighted by conviction. Proposals must also meet minimum participation thresholds before becoming eligible for funding.

This creates a very different funding philosophy:

Funding follows community conviction.

Not simply:

“Who has the most followers?”

Not simply:

“Who has the most tokens?”

And not simply:

“What does the company want to fund?”

The experiment is whether a decentralized community can collectively discover and finance things that are genuinely useful.

What Does Real Decentralization Look Like?

For us, decentralization isn’t simply putting a token on a blockchain.

The harder questions are:

  • Who decides what gets funded?
  • Who decides what is valuable?
  • Who gets governance influence?
  • How does reputation develop?
  • How can contributors earn recognition?
  • How can communities challenge bad decisions?
  • How can participants coordinate without a central authority?
  • How can we prevent wealth alone from determining governance?

These are governance problems, not merely technical problems.

SmartPushti is an attempt to experiment with some answers.

Conviction voting is one of those experiments.

Instead of making governance a competition for whoever can accumulate the most tokens, we want to explore a system where commitment, time, participation, reputation, and capital interact.

Better People, Better Decisions

This is where conviction voting becomes important.

Governance isn’t only about how many tokens someone owns.

It is about what they believe in and how strongly they are willing to commit to it.

With conviction voting, participants can support proposals with different levels of conviction. Greater conviction requires greater commitment, including longer token lock periods in SmartPushti’s current design.

This creates an important relationship between influence and commitment.

If you strongly believe a proposal will benefit the community, you can demonstrate that conviction.

If you want to change your position, you may have to give up accumulated conviction.

If you want greater influence, you have to accept greater commitment.

The objective is to make governance less about impulsive decisions and more about considered, sustained participation.
